Builds on `act-1-draft.md`. The owner didn't like the pacing of Claude's first Acts 2–3 path (one run per district, the fake-revenge scapegoat, the finale structure). Only what the owner confirmed or asked for is kept below; Claude's proposals are marked as such.

## Confirmed by the owner (15 Oct)
- **Stories between the runs.** Act 2 needs stories in between, with proper cutscenes, not just runs.
- **Oja = weed (marijuana).** The boss runs the motor park and the okada park. His other business is moving weed around Gidi through his danfo drivers and okada riders. It's shown with weight, never glamorised: drugs are cargo, never a power-up.
- **The start of Act 2.** Robbed at the end of Act 1, Tunde goes back to beg the boss. The boss, kind and loving before, is now colder and more candid: "I can help you, but the way I will help you is that you will transport for me until my money is complete." It's a 360 deal: the money never gets complete.
- **The debt is loan-shark style.** An outrageous interest rate on everything, including the bike itself, and a crazy amount added for every day Tunde doesn't pay, like the predatory lending people know from home. By the end of Act 2 it's clear he's probably never getting out.
- **The company bike.** The boss gives a bike for each run and takes it back when the run is done. Snide line from the boss: "If you like, make dem steal this one too." Outside runs Tunde is on foot: danfo, the ride-hailing app (his own money), stealing a bike or car, or buying his own.
- **Tunde can buy bikes and cars,** so the world needs vehicle dealers (tokunbo car lots, bike sellers).
- **Danfo interiors and danfo crowds** are needed, because Tunde rides them.
- **The complaint.** A plot point in Act 2: Tunde goes to the boss and says, "Abeg, this money is not going down."
- **The click.** In an Act 2 scene, someone tells Tunde he's a fool: "How did you not know how your bike got stolen?" That's when it clicks, and the game replays what happened.
- **The heart of the story.** After Tunde learns how he was made into what he is, he tries to help other boys out of the same trap.
- **Dropped:** the yahoo friend hacking the boss's phone (too far-fetched).
- **Every interior is real,** for now. The owner's choice; to revisit if scope bites.
- **Basics needed early:**
  - a phone, with a betting app and a ride-hailing app
  - walk-in betting shops
  - walk-in brothels (off screen, per the Act 1 rules)
  - clothes, phones and other things to buy

  Everything touches Tunde's money.
- **Dialogue-first prototype.** Before any cutscene, write each scene's dialogue as a comic or text version to check the story works (see `gidi-performance-capture.md`: Ink).
- **Act 1 is the first target.** Act 2 will take far longer to build (maybe a year).

## The ending (owner, 15 Oct): the standoff
A war-like face-off: the boss on one side with his boys, Tunde on the other with the boys he saved behind him, and the two sides clash. That's the end of the story.

**Claude's notes to make it land:**
- **The boys behind Tunde are exactly the boys he freed,** bought out of their debts with his own pocket money. Free three and three stand behind him; free twenty and it's an army. The player's whole game, visible in one line.
- **The boss's side are boys still in debt,** the way Tunde was in Act 1, and some are faces the player knows: a rider from a fare, a boy who carried oja with him. The climax is the boss's victims about to fight each other, and only the boss wins that fight.
- **The strongest version:** before anyone swings, Tunde speaks to the boss's boys, because he carried the same debt. How many cross over depends on how the player played: boys freed, reputation, how he treated his riders.
  - **Played well:** the boss's boys walk away one by one and he's left alone. The defeat is that nobody will fight for him any more.
  - **Played badly:** few cross over. A real fight with real losses, including faces the player recognises.
- **Where:** the boss's motor park, where it started, or Ladi Lane market, full circle with the opening chase.
- **Scope:** a standoff of 20–30 characters plus a fight is very buildable. A hundred-person battle is much harder (crowds, animation). The "walk away" version is cheaper as well as stronger.

## Claude's proposal: the 360 deal in play
**The rigged ledger on the phone.** Runs knock the number down a little; the interest and daily penalties push it back up. The player slowly sees it never really moves. So the mechanic is the 360 deal, and it plants the betrayal before anyone says it.

**Two wallets, so money matters all game:**
1. The boss's ledger: rigged, never really goes down.
2. Tunde's own pocket: fares, tips, side jobs, betting wins. Spent on clothes, the phone, betting, nights out, ride-hailing and vehicles.

**How helping the boys ties into the story.** The boss's real income is the debts of all his boys. In Act 3, Tunde uses his own pocket money to buy out other boys' debts. Every boy freed becomes Tunde's rider, takes income away from the boss, and stands behind Tunde at the standoff. The economy, the theme and the ending are the same thing.

## How long the game is (Claude)
Length comes from arithmetic, so it can be designed on purpose.
- **Act 1 free roam** ≈ the number of fares needed × the average fare time. An okada crosses a district in about 3–5 minutes on our map (from the city living tests); with the pickup, a fare is roughly 4–6 minutes.
- **So pick the target length first, then set the debt to match.** For example: about 2 hours of fares at ~5 minutes each is ~25 fares. Set the debt to roughly 25 average fares, adjusted for banned-zone bonuses, fines and spending. Then add ~30–45 minutes of story missions and cutscenes.
- **The same sum works for Act 2:** story missions × their length, plus the free-roam time the economy asks for.
